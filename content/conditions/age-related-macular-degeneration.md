## What is age-related macular degeneration?

The macula is the small central area of the retina that gives us the sharp, detailed vision we use for reading, driving and recognising faces. In age-related macular degeneration (AMD) the cells of the macula are damaged over time, and central vision becomes blurred or distorted. Side (peripheral) vision is not affected, so AMD does not cause complete blindness, but it can make everyday tasks difficult.

There are two forms:

- **Dry (atrophic) AMD** is the most common. Waste deposits called drusen build up under the retina and the light-sensing cells slowly thin. Vision changes gradually over years. In advanced dry AMD, patches of retina stop working altogether (geographic atrophy).
- **Wet (neovascular) AMD** occurs when abnormal blood vessels grow under the macula and leak fluid or blood. Vision can deteriorate within days or weeks. Wet AMD is a medical priority because prompt treatment can stop the damage.

## Symptoms

- Blurred or fuzzy central vision, or difficulty reading small print even with glasses.
- Straight lines, such as door frames or text, appearing wavy, bent or broken.
- A dark, grey or blank patch in the centre of vision.
- Colours looking faded and difficulty adapting to dim light.
- Early AMD often causes no symptoms and is found at a routine eye examination.

## Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is not fully understood, but the strongest risk factors are increasing age (usually over 50), a family history of AMD and genetic factors. **Smoking** roughly doubles the risk and is the most important factor you can change. High blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, obesity, a diet low in leafy green vegetables and fish, and long-term unprotected sun exposure also contribute.

## Diagnosis

Your ophthalmologist will examine the retina through dilated pupils and use **optical coherence tomography (OCT)**, a painless scan that shows the layers of the retina in fine detail. Retinal photography, fundus autofluorescence and, when needed, OCT angiography or fluorescein angiography help distinguish dry from wet AMD and guide treatment. An Amsler grid is a simple chart you can use at home to check each eye for new distortion between visits.

## Treatment at QEI

**Wet AMD** is treated with injections of anti-VEGF medicines into the eye, which stop abnormal vessels leaking and growing. Injections are given in the clinic under local anaesthetic, usually monthly to begin with and then at longer intervals tailored to your response. Most patients maintain or improve their vision when treatment is started promptly and continued. QEI's retina specialists also take part in clinical trials of longer-acting treatments, including sustained-release delivery systems.

**Dry AMD** cannot yet be reversed, but progression can be slowed. Your ophthalmologist may recommend AREDS2 formulation supplements for intermediate disease, stopping smoking, a diet rich in green leafy vegetables and fish, and UV protection. Regular monitoring detects any change to the wet form early. QEI is participating in clinical trials of new treatments for geographic atrophy.

For people with reduced central vision, low-vision aids, magnifiers, lighting and technology can make a real difference, and we can refer you to low-vision services.

## Living with AMD

Check each eye separately with an Amsler grid every week and contact us promptly if you notice new distortion or a blank patch. Keep to your review schedule even when your vision feels stable, and tell your family: first-degree relatives should have regular eye checks from their 50s.
