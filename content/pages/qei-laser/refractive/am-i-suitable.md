faq:
  - q: "Is there an upper age limit for laser eye surgery?"
    a: |
      No fixed limit. Laser can be performed at any adult age provided the eye is healthy, but from the mid-40s the natural lens begins to lose its reading focus and from the 60s it may begin to cloud, so for many older patients refractive lens exchange gives a better and more durable result than laser. Your assessment will tell us which.
  - q: "I have astigmatism. Can it be corrected?"
    a: |
      Yes. All modern laser procedures correct astigmatism up to about 5 D, the toric EVO ICL corrects up to about 4 D, and toric lens implants correct astigmatism during lens exchange. Irregular astigmatism caused by keratoconus or scarring is a different matter and is treated by [QEI Laser Therapeutic](/qei-laser/therapeutic/).
  - q: "My prescription is −12. Is anything possible?"
    a: |
      Almost certainly. Prescriptions beyond about −8 to −10 D are usually treated with the EVO ICL, which is approved to −20 D and gives excellent quality of vision in high short-sightedness. Laser at these levels would remove too much cornea and compromise night vision.
  - q: "I have keratoconus. Can I have laser to get rid of my glasses?"
    a: |
      Not conventional laser vision correction, which would weaken an already weak cornea. Keratoconus is treated with cross-linking, CAIRS and topography-guided laser through QEI Laser Therapeutic, and in some stabilised eyes the ICL can reduce dependence on glasses afterwards. See [keratoconus treatment](/qei-laser/therapeutic/keratoconus-corneal-cross-linking/).
  - q: "Can I have surgery while pregnant or breastfeeding?"
    a: |
      We ask you to wait. Hormonal changes alter the prescription and the tear film, and some of the drops used after surgery are best avoided. Assessment is fine at any time; surgery is scheduled at least three months after breastfeeding has finished.
  - q: "I have diabetes. Am I excluded?"
    a: |
      Not if it is well controlled and there is no diabetic eye disease. Poorly controlled diabetes slows corneal healing and makes the prescription fluctuate, so we ask for a stable HbA1c and a recent retinal check.
  - q: "I had laser years ago and my vision has drifted. Can it be redone?"
    a: |
      Often yes. After a full assessment of the corneal thickness and shape, a surface laser touch-up, a flap lift, the ICL or, in older eyes, lens exchange may be appropriate. Bring any records of your original treatment.

## The quick check

You are very likely to be suitable for one of our procedures if you can say yes to all of these:

- I am 18 or over (ideally 21 or over).
- My glasses or contact lens prescription has not changed by more than about 0.50 D in the past year.
- My eyes are healthy: no keratoconus, glaucoma, cataract, retinal disease or recurrent eye infections.
- I am in good general health, not pregnant or breastfeeding, and have no uncontrolled autoimmune disease.
- I understand that the aim is to reduce dependence on glasses and contact lenses, and that no procedure can guarantee freedom from glasses for every task for life.

None of these on its own rules you out; they simply tell us which options to consider.

## What we measure and why

A laser vision assessment at QEI Laser Refractive takes up to two hours and is performed by the surgeon who would carry out your treatment, supported by our orthoptists and optometrists. It includes:

- **Refraction**, with and without dilating drops, to establish your true prescription.
- **Corneal tomography** (Pentacam or equivalent), which maps the shape and thickness of the front and back of the cornea and is the single most important test for excluding keratoconus and confirming that enough tissue remains after laser. We look for at least 250 to 300 micrometres of untouched cornea after treatment and avoid altering more than about 40 per cent of the corneal thickness.
- **Wavefront analysis** of the optics of the whole eye for customised laser planning.
- **Epithelial mapping** with OCT to detect subtle surface irregularity.
- **Tear film assessment**, including tear break-up time and meibomian gland imaging, because an unhealthy tear film blurs the measurements and slows recovery. Our [Dry Eye Clinic](/dry-eye-clinic/) can treat it before surgery.
- **Pupil size** in dim light, which influences the size of the treatment zone and the risk of night glare.
- **Anterior chamber depth, endothelial cell count and lens measurements** when the ICL or lens exchange is being considered.
- **Dilated examination of the retina and optic nerve** to exclude conditions that would change the plan.

## Reasons we may recommend against surgery

We turn away a proportion of people who ask for laser, because the best outcome for them is no surgery or a different procedure. Common reasons include:

- keratoconus or a suspicious corneal shape on tomography;
- a prescription that is still changing, including during pregnancy and breastfeeding;
- severe dry eye that has not responded to treatment;
- active or recurrent eye disease such as herpetic keratitis, uveitis or uncontrolled glaucoma;
- uncontrolled autoimmune or connective tissue disease, or medicines that impair healing (for example isotretinoin in the past six months);
- a cataract that would be better treated as cataract surgery;
- expectations that no procedure can meet.

If we advise against surgery we will explain why in writing, so that you can seek a second opinion with the full picture.

## Preparing for your assessment

Contact lenses change the shape of the cornea and must be left out before your measurements: soft lenses for at least one week, toric soft lenses for two weeks, and rigid or orthokeratology lenses for four weeks or longer. Your pupils will be dilated, so you will not be able to drive home and will be light-sensitive for a few hours. Bring your current glasses, any previous prescriptions and a list of medicines.
