QEI Laser Refractive

Refractive lens exchange (lens replacement surgery) in Brisbane

Refractive lens exchange (RLE) replaces the eye's natural lens with a premium artificial lens chosen for your eyes and your life: trifocal, extended-depth-of-focus, toric or monofocal. It is the procedure that corrects presbyopia, the need for reading glasses that arrives in the mid-40s, as well as high long-sightedness and short-sightedness, and because the natural lens has been replaced you will never develop a cataract.

At a glance

Corrects
Long-sightedness and short-sightedness of any degree, astigmatism, and presbyopia (the need for reading glasses). Removes the possibility of cataract later in life
How
The natural lens is removed by ultrasound (phacoemulsification), with femtosecond laser assistance where appropriate, and replaced with a trifocal, extended-depth-of-focus, toric or monofocal lens implant matched to your eye
Procedure time
About 15 to 20 minutes per eye, usually one eye at a time about a week apart, with sedation from our anaesthetist
Recovery
Vision improves within a day or two; drops for four weeks; the brain adapts to multifocal optics over weeks to months
Best for
People from their mid-40s onwards who want to reduce their dependence on reading and distance glasses, especially those with long-sightedness or early lens changes
Guide price (2026)
From $6,000 per eye, all-inclusive. When a cataract is present, the operation is cataract surgery and Medicare and health funds contribute

What is refractive lens exchange?

Inside every eye is a natural lens that fine-tunes focus. From our mid-40s it stiffens and loses the ability to change focus (presbyopia), which is why reading glasses appear; later it clouds, which is cataract. Refractive lens exchange removes the natural lens before it has become a cataract and replaces it with an artificial intraocular lens (IOL) chosen to give you the vision you want. It is the same operation as cataract surgery, performed through a 2 millimetre incision under local anaesthetic and sedation, and it takes about 15 to 20 minutes per eye.

Because the new lens is selected and calculated for your eye, lens exchange can correct large amounts of long-sightedness, short-sightedness and astigmatism in a single step. And because the natural lens is gone, the result is permanent and a cataract can never develop.

Which lens implant?

The lens implant, not the surgery, determines the result, and choosing it is the most important conversation you will have with your surgeon.

  • Trifocal lenses provide three focal points (distance, arm's length and reading) and offer the highest chance of complete spectacle independence. Halos around lights at night are common at first and fade with adaptation.
  • Extended-depth-of-focus (EDOF) lenses stretch a single focus across distance and intermediate range, with fewer night-vision symptoms than trifocals. Most people need only light reading glasses for small print.
  • Toric versions of each of these correct astigmatism at the same time.
  • Monofocal lenses give the sharpest, most natural distance vision with reading glasses for near, or can be set for 'monovision' with one eye focused slightly nearer.

We use lenses from the major manufacturers, selected on the measurements of your eye, the health of your macula and cornea, your night-driving needs and your occupation.

Who is lens exchange for?

Lens exchange is the procedure of choice when:

  • you are in your mid-40s or older and have become dependent on reading glasses, bifocals or progressive lenses;
  • you are long-sighted, particularly beyond about +4 D, where laser is less predictable and the ICL less suitable;
  • you have early changes in the natural lens that make laser a poor investment;
  • you want a single, permanent solution that also removes the prospect of cataract surgery later.

It is generally not recommended below the mid-40s, or for highly short-sighted eyes with a long axial length, because of the higher risk of retinal detachment in those eyes; the ICL is usually better for them. Multifocal lenses are also avoided when there is macular disease, advanced glaucoma or significant corneal irregularity, because those conditions reduce the quality of vision the lens can deliver.

What happens on the day

  1. Your eyes are measured with optical biometry and the lens power calculated for each eye. Toric and some premium lenses are ordered specifically for you.
  2. On the day you attend South Brisbane Day Hospital, co-located with the QEI Clinic. An anaesthetist provides sedation; the eye is numbed with drops or a small injection.
  3. Through a 2 millimetre incision the natural lens is broken up with ultrasound and removed. Where it adds precision, the femtosecond laser performs the incisions and lens opening.
  4. The folded implant is injected into the lens capsule, where it unfolds and centres itself. The incision seals without stitches.
  5. You go home an hour or so later with a clear shield over the eye. The second eye is usually treated one to two weeks later.

Recovery and adaptation

Vision is usually noticeably better the next day and continues to improve over the first week. Drops are used for four weeks. You can read, watch television and use screens immediately, return to desk work within a few days and drive when your surgeon confirms you meet the standard, often within a week. With trifocal and EDOF lenses your brain learns to use the new optics over a period of weeks to a few months (neuroadaptation), during which halos diminish and near vision improves.

Results and risks

Modern lens surgery is safe and highly predictable. Serious complications are rare, but because it is an intraocular operation we discuss each of the following with you:

  • Posterior capsule opacification, a clouding of the membrane behind the implant that develops in a proportion of eyes over the years and is cleared in minutes with a painless YAG laser treatment.
  • Residual prescription (refractive surprise) in a small percentage of eyes, corrected with glasses or a fine-tuning laser treatment.
  • Halos and glare with multifocal optics, common early and usually fading; lens exchange for intolerance is needed in fewer than 1 in 100.
  • Swelling of the macula (cystoid macular oedema) in about 1 to 2 per cent, treated with drops.
  • Retinal detachment, uncommon but more likely in highly short-sighted eyes, which is why we examine the retina carefully and counsel you on warning symptoms.
  • Infection inside the eye (endophthalmitis), very rare, in the order of 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 2,000, treated urgently.
  • Loss of natural focusing, permanent, which is why lens exchange is reserved for eyes that have already lost it.

Medicare, health funds and cost

When there is no cataract, lens exchange is an elective procedure and is not covered by Medicare. Our guide price is from $6,000 per eye, all-inclusive of surgeon, hospital, anaesthetist, premium lens and 12 months of aftercare. If your assessment shows an early cataract, the operation becomes cataract surgery: Medicare and, if you have hospital cover, your health fund contribute to the surgery and hospital costs, and you pay only the gap and any premium lens component. See costs and payment.

Questions about Refractive lens exchange

Is refractive lens exchange the same as cataract surgery?

Technically yes. The operation is identical to modern cataract surgery, which is the most frequently performed operation in Australia. The difference is the reason for doing it: in cataract surgery the natural lens has become cloudy, whereas in refractive lens exchange a still-clear lens is replaced purely to correct vision. Because the lens is replaced, you can never develop a cataract afterwards. Our surgeons perform the same procedure, with the same lens technology, for both.

Will I be completely free of glasses?

With a trifocal lens most people manage distance, computer and reading tasks without glasses, and large studies report more than nine in ten patients achieving spectacle independence for everyday activities. Extended-depth-of-focus lenses give excellent distance and intermediate vision with a little less near vision, so thin reading glasses may be needed for very small print or dim light. Monofocal lenses give the crispest distance vision with reading glasses for near. Your surgeon will match the lens to your priorities and be honest about what each can and cannot deliver.

What are halos and will I get them?

Trifocal and, to a lesser extent, extended-depth-of-focus lenses split light between distance and near focus, and most people notice rings or halos around lights at night in the first months. For the great majority these fade as the brain adapts and become unimportant; fewer than one in a hundred people find them intolerable enough to have the lens exchanged. If you drive long distances at night for a living, your surgeon may recommend an extended-depth-of-focus or monofocal lens instead.

Can I have lens exchange in my thirties?

Usually not. Below the mid-40s the natural lens still focuses for reading, and replacing it means giving that up. Laser vision correction or the ICL are better options for younger eyes. In highly short-sighted eyes lens exchange also carries a higher risk of retinal detachment, so it is generally avoided in younger short-sighted patients.

Can the result be fine-tuned afterwards?

Yes. If a small residual prescription remains after the eye has healed, it can usually be corrected with a brief laser treatment (TransPRK or LASIK) at three months, which is included in our first-year aftercare.
