QEI Laser Refractive

TransPRK laser eye surgery in Brisbane

TransPRK (transepithelial photorefractive keratectomy) is the most advanced form of surface laser. In a single, no-touch step the excimer laser removes the thin surface layer of the cornea and reshapes the tissue beneath it. Nothing touches the eye but light. There is no flap and no incision, which makes it the safest choice for thinner corneas, contact sports and people prone to dry eye.

What is TransPRK?

The cornea is covered by a thin layer of surface cells, the epithelium, which regrows within days if it is removed. Surface laser procedures take advantage of this: the epithelium is removed, the excimer laser reshapes the corneal tissue beneath it, and the epithelium heals back over the new shape. Unlike LASIK, no flap is cut and no femtosecond laser incision is made.

TransPRK is the modern, single-step version of this idea. The excimer laser first removes the epithelium with a precisely calculated ablation and then, without pause, reshapes the cornea to correct your prescription. The whole treatment takes less than a minute per eye and nothing touches the eye except light. There is no alcohol, no brush and no blade.

Why surface laser can be the safest option

  • Maximum corneal strength. With no flap, the deeper layers of the cornea are untouched. Surface laser leaves the strongest possible cornea and has the lowest risk of ectasia of any laser technique, which makes it the preferred option for thinner corneas or when the treatment would alter a larger proportion of corneal thickness.
  • No flap complications, ever. People in contact sports, martial arts, the defence forces or emergency services, and anyone at risk of eye injury, avoid the small lifetime risk of a flap being dislodged.
  • Kinder to the tear film. Fewer corneal nerves are cut than in LASIK, so long-term dry eye is less common.
  • Faster healing than older PRK. Studies comparing single-step TransPRK with alcohol-assisted PRK found the surface healed in about 3.2 days rather than 4.6, with better vision in the first week and less early haze.

The trade-off: a slower recovery

Because the surface layer has to regrow, vision is blurry and the eye is uncomfortable for three to five days, and the final result takes longer to arrive than with LASIK or CLEAR. Plan for around a week away from work, avoid driving until your surgeon confirms your vision is adequate (often one to two weeks), and expect your vision to keep improving for one to three months. Drops are used for several weeks, and for higher corrections a short application of mitomycin C during surgery reduces the risk of haze.

Who is TransPRK for?

TransPRK corrects short-sightedness, long-sightedness and astigmatism in a similar range to LASIK. It is often the recommended procedure when tomography shows a cornea that is on the thinner side, when a flap is undesirable because of sport or occupation, when there is a history of dry eye, or when a previous procedure needs fine-tuning. Some people simply prefer the peace of mind of a flap-free treatment and accept the slower recovery. See am I suitable?.

What happens on the day

  1. Anaesthetic drops numb the eye and a small speculum keeps the eyelids open.
  2. You look at a fixation light while the laser removes the epithelium and reshapes the cornea, usually 30 to 60 seconds per eye. An eye tracker follows any movement.
  3. For larger corrections a sponge soaked in mitomycin C is applied briefly to reduce haze, then rinsed.
  4. A soft bandage contact lens is placed and drops are applied. Both eyes are usually treated in one visit.
  5. Someone drives you home; rest, sunglasses and the drops and pain relief we provide make the first days manageable.

Results and risks

At six months and beyond, TransPRK produces the same excellent visual results as LASIK, and its long-term safety profile is arguably the best of all laser techniques. Risks include delayed healing of the surface, temporary haze, glare and halos in the early months, under- or over-correction that may need an enhancement, and, rarely, infection during the first days before the surface has healed, which is why the drops are so important. See compare the procedures and costs and payment.

Questions about TransPRK

How is TransPRK different from PRK?

Both treat the surface of the cornea without a flap. In conventional PRK the surgeon removes the epithelium with a dilute alcohol solution or a soft brush before the laser reshapes the cornea. In TransPRK the laser removes the epithelium itself, in the same continuous treatment, so nothing touches the eye. Comparative studies show TransPRK heals faster (about 3 days versus 4 to 5), with less early discomfort and haze, while the final vision at 6 to 12 months is the same.

Why would I choose TransPRK over LASIK?

Because there is no flap, TransPRK preserves more of the cornea's strength, carries no risk of flap-related problems and has a lower risk of long-term dry eye. It is the safer choice for thinner or slightly irregular corneas, for people in contact sports or the defence forces, and for those who would find a flap unacceptable. The trade-off is a slower, less comfortable recovery.

How much discomfort should I expect?

The first two to three days are the hardest: expect grittiness, watering, light sensitivity and a burning sensation while the surface heals under the bandage contact lens. We provide anaesthetic and anti-inflammatory drops, pain relief and cold packs, and most people describe it as uncomfortable rather than painful. By day four or five the surface has healed and comfort returns quickly.

What is corneal haze?

Haze is a faint cloudiness in the healing cornea that can occur after surface laser, mainly with higher corrections. Modern lasers, single-step TransPRK and the use of mitomycin C for larger treatments have made significant haze uncommon, and it usually fades with time and anti-inflammatory drops.
