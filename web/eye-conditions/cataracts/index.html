Cataracts

As the eye ages it is very common for the lens inside the eye to become cloudy. This cloudy lens is called a cataract. Modern cataract surgery replaces it with a clear artificial lens and is the most commonly performed operation in the world.

At a glance

  • A cataract is a clouding of the eye's natural lens, usually due to age.
  • Symptoms develop gradually: blur, glare, halos and poor night vision.
  • Surgery is a day procedure under local anaesthetic, performed at South Brisbane Day Hospital.
  • The timing is your choice: surgery is recommended when the cataract interferes with your life.
Seek urgent care if you notice:
  • After cataract surgery: increasing pain, redness or a sudden drop in vision (call the clinic immediately)
  • Sudden loss of vision at any time

Call us on 07 3239 5000 during clinic hours, or go to your nearest emergency department. See emergency eye care.

What is a cataract?

The lens inside the eye focuses light onto the retina. With age it is very common for the lens to become cloudy; this cloudy lens is called a cataract. When the lens becomes opaque the amount of light passing through it is reduced and scattered, and the image cannot be correctly focused on the retina. Early on the change is gradual and you may not notice it. Usually both eyes are affected, though one may be worse than the other.

Symptoms

  • Gradually blurred, hazy or dim vision.
  • Glare, halos or streaks around lights, and difficulty driving at night.
  • Colours appearing faded or yellowed.
  • Frequent changes in your glasses prescription.
  • Needing brighter light to read.

Causes and risk factors

The exact mechanism is not fully understood, but the factors most strongly associated with cataract are:

  • Age. Some degree of cataract is found in most people over 70 or 80, and it is not uncommon in people in their 50s and 60s.
  • Sunlight exposure (UVB). There is a strong relationship between lifetime UV exposure and later cataract, which is one reason sunglasses matter in Queensland.
  • Other factors including diabetes, smoking, long-term steroid use, previous eye injury or surgery, and a family history. Cataracts are also more common where malnutrition is prevalent.

Diagnosis

A cataract is diagnosed with a slit-lamp examination of the eye. Before surgery, precise measurements of the eye (biometry) are taken to calculate the power of the artificial lens, and the retina is examined to make sure no other condition is affecting your vision.

Treatment at QEI

A change of glasses from your optometrist may help early on, but as the cataract progresses the only way to restore vision is surgery. Cataract surgery removes the cloudy lens and replaces it with a clear acrylic lens implant. Modern surgery uses very small sutureless incisions, a specialised phacoemulsification machine to dissolve the cataract, and lenses that unfold inside the eye.

QEI specialises in advanced cataract surgery with internationally trained subspecialist surgeons using the latest technology and equipment, including femtosecond laser assistance and premium lens options where appropriate. Surgery is performed as a day procedure at South Brisbane Day Hospital, co-located with our Woolloongabba clinic. Whatever vision will return with removal of the cataract today will be the same in five years' time, so the operation is elective and the timing is your decision.

Recovery

Most people notice clearer vision within a day or two and return to normal activities within a week. You will use antibiotic and anti-inflammatory drops for a few weeks, avoid rubbing the eye and swimming, and attend follow-up appointments. The second eye is usually operated on a few weeks after the first. Serious complications such as infection or retinal detachment are rare but require urgent attention; contact the clinic immediately if you have increasing pain, redness or a drop in vision.

Frequently asked questions

When should I have cataract surgery?

A cataract almost never damages the eye; it simply blocks vision progressively as it becomes cloudier. The time to remove it is when it interferes with your vision enough that you can no longer do the things you want to do, such as driving, reading or working. Your ophthalmologist will help you decide.

What does surgery involve?

Cataract surgery is a day procedure performed under local anaesthetic, usually with light sedation. Through a tiny sutureless incision the cloudy lens is dissolved with an ultrasound probe (phacoemulsification) and a folded artificial lens is placed inside the eye, where it unfolds. The operation itself typically takes 15 to 20 minutes.

Will I still need glasses?

That depends on the lens chosen. A standard monofocal lens gives clear distance vision with reading glasses. Toric lenses correct astigmatism, and multifocal or extended depth of focus lenses reduce dependence on glasses for most tasks. Your surgeon will discuss which option suits your eyes and lifestyle.

Can a cataract come back?

No, but months or years later the membrane behind the artificial lens can become cloudy (posterior capsule opacification). This is easily treated in the clinic with a painless laser procedure that takes a few minutes.

Concerned about cataracts?
